How It Works

The data

This app uses 2,507 public runner performances from Superior 100 races held from 2014 through 2025, excluding 2020. The records contain passage times at up to 14 aid stations, from Split Rock through the finish.

A person who raced in several years contributes one performance for each year. Missing splits are allowed, so not every performance contains every aid station.

Building the matching group

Your aid station selections define a fresh historical cohort for every upcoming station. A performance must match the latest sighting within 10 minutes. When enough records are available, it must also match the previous sighting within 10 minutes.

If matching both recent sightings leaves fewer than 20 candidates, the app matches the latest sighting only. Every historical performance that meets the matching and validity requirements contributes equally to the results. At least five matching performances are required to show results.

Turning history into clock times

The app measures how long each cohort member took to travel from your runner's latest known aid station to the upcoming station. It adds those historical travel times to your runner's actual sighting time.

This means the app does not copy a historical runner's absolute time of day. A runner seen at Sawbill at 1 PM is compared using historical Sawbill-to-Oberg travel durations, all starting from 1 PM on the current race clock.

Station-pair-specific statistical bounds remove implausible segment durations before the results are calculated.

Reading the results

  • Fastest is the shortest valid travel time in the cohort matching your input times.
  • Typical is the median travel time. Half of the cohort was faster and half was slower.
  • Slower is the 90th-percentile travel time. Nine in ten cohort members were this fast or faster.

Slower is not the slowest possible result. One in ten matching performances took longer. The app displays the number of historical performances used for each station.

Multiple sightings

Every new sighting rebuilds the cohort. The latest one or two sightings define the match, with the most recent race information taking priority.

The newest actual sighting also becomes the starting point for every remaining travel-time calculation. This prevents an older absolute clock time from producing an impossible upcoming segment.

How the method was tested

The model was tested with leave-one-year-out backtesting. For each test, one race year was hidden, the cohorts were built from every other year, and each hidden runner's next aid station was evaluated using only their sightings up to that point.

The test produced 23,935 next-station results from 24,160 eligible transitions, for 99.1% coverage. The displayed Fastest time was still later than the runner's actual passage in 3.5% of those results.

This is why Fastest is a historical cohort statistic, not a guaranteed earliest arrival.

Important limitations

  • These statistics describe historical cohorts, not your specific runner. Weather, injury, aid station stops, and race-day decisions can change the result.
  • If missing your runner is unacceptable, do not treat Fastest as a guaranteed safe arrival time. Account for your own driving, parking, and walking margin.
  • Public timing records can contain missing or incorrect splits. The outlier filter reduces obvious errors but cannot identify every data problem.
  • Aid station names and course distances have changed slightly across years. The model uses recorded passage times and station order.
